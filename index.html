About

I'm finishing my M.S. in Business Analytics at Columbia University (December 2026) and looking for a research assistant position in clinical risk prediction, LLM evaluation in medicine, or biostatistics, starting January 2027.

Most of my research so far comes from one question: when should a clinician trust a model? In my capstone at Columbia University Irving Medical Center, I built center-specific surgical risk models for high-risk neonates with congenital heart disease, where national registry scores work least well. Along the way I found that a single LLM used for chart abstraction quietly pulled rare anatomies toward common diagnoses, and I replaced it with a multi-LLM consensus that sends the hard cases to clinicians. At Meituan I ran into the same problem from the other side: an LLM judge that rewarded confident prose even when the numbers were wrong.

I care about calibration and uncertainty as much as discrimination, about evaluation designs that reflect how a model will actually be used, and about randomized experiments as the final test of whether a tool changes decisions. I work in Python, R, and SQL. The best way to reach me is by email.

Research

  1. Jan – May 2026

    Clinical AI for Surgical Risk in Neonatal Congenital Heart Disease · Research Capstone, Columbia University Irving Medical CenterNew York · Advisor: Nimrod Goldshtrom, MD, MS

    • Center-specific risk model. National STS and PC4 scores are least reliable for the highest-risk neonates. I framed mortality and length-of-stay prediction on a 20-year cohort of 1,500+ neonatal cardiac surgeries, used temporal validation to account for practice drift, and kept penalized logistic regression as the primary model after gradient boosting's advantage failed to hold across eras.
    • Multi-LLM chart abstraction. Checking LLM-extracted STS codes against clinician abstraction showed that a single model pulled rare anatomies toward common diagnoses and changed its answer when a note was reordered. A cross-family LLM panel with evidence quoting and retrieved code definitions fixed most of this; because the models' errors were correlated on the rarest codes, those are always routed to clinician review.
    • Uncertainty-aware reporting. Each prediction comes with a conformal interval, its main drivers, and the number of comparable past cases, so clinicians can see when the model is extrapolating. The tool was built for the hospital's weekly multidisciplinary case conference.

  2. May – Aug 2026

    Evaluating LLM Agents for Automated Analytical Reporting · AI Analytics Intern, MeituanBeijing

    • Stage-level evaluation. End-to-end checks could not say which step of an agent pipeline had failed, so I scored SQL correctness, extraction completeness, cross-source stitching, and metric consistency separately. Tracing showed many errors came from metrics defined differently across sources; a shared metric dictionary cut recurring report errors by 24.5%.
    • Auditing the LLM judge. Calibrating the judge against human labels showed it favored fluent, confident reports even when the numbers were wrong. I moved numeric checks to code, limited the judge to a fixed rubric, and kept a rotating human audit sample.
    • Packaged stable workflows into reusable agent skills used across teams, saving about 10 hours of analysis a week.
